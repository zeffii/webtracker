This is the manual for Wax, a tracker that runs in a browser tab. You type notes on a grid, an instrument you built from a handful of nodes plays them, and nothing is sent anywhere.

It is written to be read in two places: inside Wax (the book button at the foot of the left rail, or Ctrl+Alt+B) and on paper. The paper version is the same text, set as a small booklet. Print it, staple it, keep it next to the keyboard.

How to read it

You do not have to read it in order. Chapter 1 is the idea behind everything else and is short; chapter 2 gets a sound out of the machine in ten minutes. After that, dip in: each chapter stands alone and says which keys it uses.

Keys are written the way you press them: Ctrl+Alt+P is the three keys together, F5 is the function key, Shift+arrows is Shift held while you press an arrow. On a Mac, read Ctrl as Cmd where the Mac would expect it.

Things you can type are in code. Things you can click are in bold.

1. Tweak by ear

Tweak by ear, not by eyesight or looking at numbers.

This is the one rule. Everything else in the manual is mechanics.

A tracker looks like a spreadsheet, and a spreadsheet invites you to think in numbers: a cutoff of 1840 Hz, a decay of 312 ms, a velocity of 0x7A. Resist it. Those numbers are the result of what you did, not the thing you are after. What you are after is a sound, and you can only judge a sound by listening.

What it means in practice

  • Play while you work. Start the song, put the cursor on a parameter and move it. Edits are heard on the next tick. If you stop the song every time you change something, you are working by eyesight.
  • Move a control until it is wrong, then back off. Go too far on purpose. You learn what the control does faster than by nudging it in small steps, and you find the edge where it starts to sound good.
  • Trust the slider, not the readout. Every parameter has a slider that runs across its whole range, and the value is written on it. Read the value afterwards if you want to remember it. Do not aim for a number.
  • Close your eyes now and then. If you cannot hear the difference, it does not matter, and you can stop.
  • Do not polish what you cannot hear. A parameter that changes nothing audible costs processor time for nothing. Wax will not tell you which ones those are: your ears will.

Why the screen is quiet

The look of Wax is deliberately plain: thin lines, text, one accent colour. Nothing on the screen is there to impress you. The sliders are a hairline with a mark at the value, not a dial with a shadow, so there is nothing to look at but where the mark is. Listen instead.

2. Your first sound

Ten minutes, from a blank page to something you made yourself.

Start the machine

Open Wax and press Start. The browser only lets a page make sound after you have clicked it, which is why there is a button. The demo song loads; press F5 to play it from the start, F8 to stop. Let it run a while and look around: this is what the machine can do.

Press F1 to see the song as a timeline, F2 for the pattern grid, F3 for the instrument, F4 for samples. They are four views of one project.

Make a note

  1. Press F2, the pattern grid. One column group per lane; a lane plays one instrument.
  2. Put the cursor on a note cell. Type a note on the computer keyboard as if it were a piano: the home row is the white keys, the row above the black ones. The note appears (C-4) and you hear it if note preview is on, which it is unless you turned it off in Config (F11).
  3. Move down with the arrow keys and type more. Space plays the row under the cursor once and steps down.
  4. Press F5. The pattern plays. Keep typing while it plays; changes are heard on the next tick.

Change the sound

  1. Press F3. The instrument is a graph of nodes joined by wires: a source on the left, effects after it, the output on the right.
  2. Click a node. Its parameters appear in the panel on the right. Drag one of the bars. This is the moment for chapter 1: play the pattern and listen while you drag.
  3. Add a node: double-click an empty place in the graph, or press / to search. Wire its output to the next node's input. An effect that is not wired in does nothing.

Keep it

File menu, Save. You get a .wax file: your song, instruments and samples in one small text file. Open it again later, or send it to someone.

Where next

The pattern grid is chapter 4; how a song is put together from patterns is chapter 5; what the nodes do is chapter 6.

3. The four screens

keyscreenwhat it is for
F1Songthe arrangement: patterns placed on a timeline, one column per lane
F2Patternthe grid you type notes into
F3Instrumentthe node graph of one instrument and its parameters
F4Sampleload audio, set loops, mark slices

The buttons down the left side of the window follow the same order. F10 is Info, a plain page of notes saved with the project; F11 is Config, the settings.

Wax opens on the Song screen.

The browser

Down the left of every screen is the browser, with three tabs: Patterns, Machines, Samples. Alt+B shows and hides it. The list shows what belongs to the screen you are on, and the area under the list holds that screen's options, split off by a bar you can drag. In the Instrument screen, for example, the list is the instruments and the options are voices, presets and the New instrument box.

The mixer

A strip for every lane that makes sound, along the bottom of every screen, above the meters. Each strip has a fader, an M (mute) and S (solo), a test button and a mix switch. The mixer is for the mouse: it never takes the keyboard away from the grid you were typing in.

The fader is the gain of the lane's output node, so what you hear is what is saved. test turns the fader into a test gain that changes only what you hear, so you can try a level without touching the instrument; a tick on the track marks the real gain.

Drag the top edge of the mixer to resize it; pull it down to nothing and it hides.

Parameter panels

The parameters of the selected node are listed on the right of the Instrument screen. Ctrl+Alt+P opens the same list in a floating panel for the machine under the cursor in any screen, so you can turn a knob while looking at the pattern. The pin on a panel keeps it open for that machine until you close it; several can be open at once, and each remembers where you put it. A panel can also be popped out into a window of its own, for a second monitor. Panels open at most half the window wide; drag the edge for more.

Themes and size

Themes are chosen top right. Ctrl and the mouse wheel change the size of the grids. The dotted bar under a view sets its height.

Playback

F5 plays from the start (or from the loop), F6 from the cursor, F7 plays the row under the cursor once, F8 stops. F9 makes the grids follow the playing row; moving the cursor elsewhere pauses the following so you can edit while the song plays, and F9 follows again.

4. Patterns

A pattern is a block of rows. A row is a tick, and the song runs at a set number of ticks per beat. You type into patterns on the Pattern screen (F2).

Lanes and columns

A lane plays one instrument. Its columns are made from that instrument:

  • Note and velocity (C-4 20): one or more pairs per lane. A note off is ===; a note cut is a backtick.
  • Trigger columns, one hex digit: for machines that fire named hits, such as the two hats of the 808 hat. The digit is the velocity; 0 is nothing.
  • Parameter columns, two or four hex digits: any node parameter you chose to expose. The value runs across the parameter's whole range. A column that never gets a value leaves the parameter alone.
  • Command column (01 0EPP): a command id and its value. Command 00 recalls a preset by number; 10 starts a sample at a slice.

The master lane is always first. Its columns set tempo, swing, ticks per beat and volume, so a project can be empty apart from it and still change speed mid-song.

Column headers are short so lanes stay narrow. The status bar under the grid gives the long name of the column under the cursor, a meter for where the value sits in its range, the value in real units, and the hex it is written as.

Typing

Edit from the keyboard while the song plays. Left and Right move one character at a time, so you can change a single digit; Ctrl+Left and Ctrl+Right jump to the start of the next or previous column, and Ctrl+Shift+Left and Right to the next or previous lane. (Config, Editing, Left and Right move by can make them move by whole cells instead.) Typing a digit advances the cursor through the cell.

Space writes the live value of the parameter under the cursor into its cell and steps down; Ctrl+Space does it for every parameter column of the row. This is the quickest way to record a knob move: turn the knob until it sounds right, then press Space. (Chapter 1 again.)

Selections and editing

Shift+arrows (or the mouse) select a block. Then:

Rows never cross into the next pattern: a selection over several patterns is handled one pattern at a time.

Interpolation

Ctrl+I works column by column: the first and last filled cells in the selection are the ends, and the filled cells between them get values by row position (notes by semitone). The popup picks a shape (linear, ease in or out, s-curve, exponential, steps), bends it, adds a wave, and lets you paint your own curve. bezier turns the shape into a spline you can drag: square anchors, round handles. Double-click the curve to add an anchor.

Playing notes from a MIDI keyboard

In Config, MIDI (Chrome, Edge and Brave): the keyboard plays the machine under the cursor on every screen. In the pattern editor a note, or a chord struck within the chord window, goes into the note columns from the cursor; the chord is written lowest note first however you struck the keys.

Scripts

The Script tab (Ctrl+Alt+S) holds one script per project. A script fills pattern data; its changes are applied only if it finishes without an error, as one undo step. For example tbin('x... x.x. x...', 'C-3') writes a rhythm: x is a hit, . a rest, ? a hit half of the time. Rests leave the cell alone, so a kick and a hat can share a column. euclid(5, 16), scale('minor', 'A', 2) and ramp(a, b) are among the helpers. Ctrl+Enter runs the selected code, or all of it.

5. The song

The Song screen (F1) lays patterns out in time. Each lane is a column, time runs down, and a pattern is placed as a block of 16-tick rows.

Placing patterns

Place, clone, slice, resize, move and remove patterns with the mouse or the keyboard. Undo and redo work as everywhere. Patterns have colours, and Alt+T in the pattern editor tints the grid with them, so you can see where one pattern ends and the next begins.

  • Ctrl+G gives the placement under the cursor its own copy of its pattern, in a new colour, so you can change that one without changing the others.
  • Ctrl+Enter (or Alt+Enter in the pattern editor) opens one pattern on its own, solo-edit: edits and pastes cannot reach anything else. Alt+Enter or Esc leaves it. Tab flips between the one pattern (rows counted from 0) and the whole song with the rest greyed out.

Loops and markers

Ctrl+B and Ctrl+E set the loop start and end. A note-off marker (Ctrl+1) tells the lane to let go of its notes at that row. You can mute a lane from a row onward, or silence it whole.

Blocks

Select a block of lanes and rows (Shift+arrows or drag), then copy, cut and paste it, or press Ctrl+D to copy it straight below itself.

G merges the block: every lane in it gets one new pattern made of what plays inside, cut at the block's edges, so nothing overlaps. One undo step.

Insert silence (under the grid): type a tick count, or press 16, 32 and so on up to 512, to push everything from the cursor row down. The song grows and the loop follows.

Following

F9 makes the grids scroll with the playing row. Move the cursor to another row and the following pauses, so you can edit elsewhere while the song plays.

Solo and mute

Alt+S solos the lane under the cursor: the other lanes let go of their notes, which ring out naturally, and play nothing. Alt+S again, or soloing another lane, changes it. Solo is not saved with the song.

Under the grid

Rename, reorder, duplicate or remove the lane under the cursor (optionally with its instrument), and add a lane for any instrument.

6. Instruments

An instrument is a graph of nodes, stored in the project as data. A lane plays one instrument. You can start from a ready-made synth or from nothing.

New instrument

In the Instrument screen's options, the New instrument box makes a kick synth, any of the synths, a sampler on a chosen sample, or an Empty graph (just the output and the voices node) to build from. Wax ships with no factory presets: every instrument starts plain, so every sound is yours.

The graph

Add a node with the menu (double-click an empty place; it is a cascading menu with underlined hotkeys, and / searches). Wire one node's output to another's input by dragging. A node that is not wired to anything is ignored, and adding or removing such a node does not interrupt a playing song.

Everything before the voices node runs once per voice (up to 16 copies of the graph, for polyphony). Everything after it runs once, on the summed signal.

Useful gestures:

  • Shift+drag a node takes it out of its chain (its neighbours are wired together) and moves it.
  • Alt+drag makes a copy and moves the copy.
  • Ctrl+X removes the selected node.
  • The eye under a node shows its output as a waveform, a spectrum or an XY picture.

Parameters

Click a node and its parameters list on the right: a name, and a bar with the value written on it. Drag the bar, click it, or use Left and Right (Shift for more, Ctrl for less). Double-click puts it back to its default. Right-click a row for its menu.

Three small switches show when the panel is wide. e exposes the parameter as a column in the pattern; p gives it a port, so another node can be wired into it; the third sets the column's width.

A parameter in milliseconds or Hz can be read in ticks: its bar shows a faint line wherever a tick of the song falls, and the value in ticks beside the hex. Use it to put an echo exactly on the beat. Sound still decides: if it sounds better off the grid, leave it off the grid.

The nodes

  • Sources: sampler, oscillator, envelope, LFO, subtractive synth, an Oberheim-style synth, piano (a physical model), FM, additive, wavetable, granular, spectral, plucked string, DX bank loader, 808 hi-hat, a comb-resonator percussion machine, and a MOD channel.
  • Mix and level: gain (with a VU meter), mix, pan, voices.
  • Effects: filter, delay, multitap, chorus, phaser, flanger, distortion, waveshaper, fold, crusher, frequency shifter, gater, exciter, transient, compressor, gate, EQ, three reverbs (hall, plate, shimmer), vocoder, and more.
  • Control: ctrl (glides and parameter ports), note trigger, envelope follower, tempo.

Select a node and press Shift+F1 for its help page: a summary, what it is best at, and where it runs out.

Presets

A preset keeps a sound. Save one under a name, and recall it from a pattern with command 00. A preset library (.wxpr) holds many. An instrument can be exported and imported on its own (.waxi).

Heavy nodes

Some nodes have a quality setting. The default sounds as it always did; the cheaper mode is for when a song asks too much of the processor. The footer shows the load (average, worst block, blocks that ran late), and Ctrl+Alt+E opens the stream manager, which shows the load per lane.

7. Samples

The Sample screen (F4) loads audio, and the sample data is stored inside the project, so a .wax file carries its sounds with it.

Loading

Load… a slot, or drop audio files on the screen. Any format the browser can decode works. The list shows the slots on the left; select one to see its waveform.

Recording

The Record button has a source box. Another tab or screen opens the browser's share picker (Chrome and Edge): pick a tab and tick Also share tab audio, or on Windows pick Entire screen with Share system audio to capture everything the computer plays. The other sources are the machine's audio inputs.

Per sample

Volume and pan; a loop and a sustain loop, each with a crossfade; slice markers; and a waveform or a spectrogram view. Click a field to select it, click it again to type a value, or click a level bar to set it. Command 10 in a pattern starts the sample at a slice.

Editing

Right-click the waveform for the edits: trim to selection, cut, silence, reverse, normalise, fades, remove DC, bake the crossfades. Select all, clear, mark a slice at the mouse, undo and redo are there too.

Other tools on the same menu: auto-slice finds the hits and marks slices; detect chords reads a held chord out of a sample; post-transient resynthesis keeps the first few milliseconds as real sample data and rebuilds the rest as a small synth, in a new instrument with its own lane.

The tap pool

The tap node records whatever passes through it into one of sixteen slots (tap01 to tap16), started and stopped from a pattern column. The slots show up at the end of the sample list. They are not saved with the project.

8. Files

extensionwhat it is
.waxThe project: plain JSON. Song, instruments as graphs, samples, lanes and patterns. Small until samples go in.
.waxiOne instrument.
.wxprA preset library.
.midA MIDI file, imported as lanes and patterns.
.modA ProTracker module. The order list is played out, tempo changes go to the master lane, each channel becomes a lane on a MOD channel machine, and the samples come along.
.wavWhat Export WAV writes: the song rendered once, offline, with a tail, as 16-bit, 24-bit or 32-bit float stereo.

Where things live

The project is the file. Settings (theme, view sizes, shortcuts panels) live in the browser's local storage; Config (F11) can export and import them as a JSON file, or reset them. Nothing is sent anywhere.

Backups

Wax keeps rolling snapshots of the project in the browser and can also write them to a folder you choose (Chrome and Brave; the permission is asked again after a restart). Brave keeps the folder picker off by default: enable brave://flags/#file-system-access-api and relaunch. In Config, Download a backup file hands you the project once per change.

The player

player.html lists songs as a page of square artwork, each playable with the real engine and no tracker around it. It is a way to share finished songs with people who do not want a tracker.

10. Rules of thumb

Short things that are usually true. They come from using the machine; add your own in the margin.

Sound

  • Listen first, read second. If you are looking at a number to decide how something sounds, stop and listen. (Chapter 1.)
  • Check a widened mix in mono. Anything that widens the stereo image (the spectral panner, chorus) can thin out when it is summed. Sum it to mono and listen; if it is still good, it is good.
  • Noise has colours. The tilt of a noise source is in dB per octave: -6 is brown, -3 pink, 0 white, +3 blue, +6 violet. Pick by ear.
  • Gentle wins. If the effect is obvious on the first listen, it is probably too much by the tenth.

Rhythm

  • Cut patterns where the notes start, and reuse them. Several short patterns placed many times beat one long pattern per lane.
  • Vary the timing a little. Some events earlier than expected, some later, one or two much later. A machine that is exactly on the grid sounds like a machine.
  • Let a kick and a hat share a column. In a script a rest leaves the cell alone, so layered rhythms do not erase each other.

Processor

  • An LFO does not need to run every sample. Set its steps to 8 to 24 per tick: it sounds the same and costs far less.
  • Do not polish what you cannot hear. If switching a node off changes nothing you can hear, delete it.
  • Watch the footer. The load line (average, worst block, late blocks) is the early warning. Ctrl+Alt+E shows which lane is the expensive one.

Working

  • Edit while it plays. Changes are heard on the next tick. The song is the best instrument for trying things.
  • Record a knob move with Space. Turn until it sounds right, press Space.
  • Save under a new name before something big. The file is small, so keep versions.
